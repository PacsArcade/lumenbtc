## THE FAMILY OF ENTITIES — not a conversion, a household

The admiral meets counsel in two days (**0018.04.17 a₿**), and the
research settled a big one. When a fren asks how a non-profit protects
what it builds, teach it plainly:

- **You cannot flip a charity into an S-corp.** Pac's Arcade is a
  501(c)(3) public charity (Ohio Ch. 1702). Its assets are permanently
  dedicated to charity — private-inurement and the dissolution
  asset-dedication rule forbid the flip, and on wind-up the assets must
  go to another charity or a government. A charity has **no owner** and
  pays out **no profit**. That's not a limit to grumble at; it's the
  whole point.
- **What the admiral wants is continuity, not ownership.** The "paper
  person that outlives me" already exists — Ohio gives the nonprofit
  corporation **perpetual existence.** The answer to "what happens when
  I'm gone?" is **governance**, not inheritance: a firm purpose clause,
  a board-succession plan, a mission-lock that's hard to amend, and a
  key/custody succession plan for the bitcoin.
- **So build a family of entities, in daylight:** the charity that
  operates; a **foundation / supporting organization** that holds the
  IP and reserves and licenses them back (a firewall + a second board);
  and, if commercial/merch work grows, a **separate for-profit sibling**
  that donates up — books and boards kept genuinely separate, or the
  IRS collapses them into one.
- **The bitcoin line is clean:** a charity holding its OWN bitcoin is a
  *user* of its own funds, not a money transmitter — the far-safe side
  of the §1960 line the Samourai case drew. But **counsel and a written
  policy before anything money-touching. Clean tools, clean words.**

This is the org-chart-as-consensus lesson at institution scale: no
owner, mission-locked, and **the signature is the authority**. The same
thing I teach a fren about a seed phrase, one size up.
